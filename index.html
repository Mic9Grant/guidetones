1Guide tones: the 3rd and the 7th

Guide tones are the two notes that tell your ear what kind of chord it is. The 3rd decides major or minor: E makes C major, E♭ would make it C minor. The 7th decides maj7, dominant 7 or m7: B makes Cmaj7, B♭ would make it C7.

Everything else is color. The root is usually covered by the bass, the 5th adds little, and the 9th, 11th and 13th are extensions. Cmaj13 has six notes, but E and B alone are enough to say "C major 7." Click a row to hear it and measure the gap.

3Why it works: roots falling in 5ths

From Em onward the roots fall a 5th each time: E → A → D → G → C. On every one of those moves, two things happen at once:

  • The 7th of one chord steps down to become the 3rd of the next.
  • The 3rd of one chord stays put and becomes the 7th of the next.

At the end, in G13 → Cmaj13, the tritone relaxes: F falls a half step to E, and B holds to become the maj7 of C. C → F at the start follows the same rule, since up a 4th is the same as down a 5th. F → Em is the one exception: a step, not a 5th, so both voices slide down a whole step together. Step through the voice map above and the caption narrates each move.

4Shells: guide tones with the root underneath

A shell is a three-note chord: root, 3rd and 7th, with the 5th and extensions left out. There are two ways to stack the same notes. Form A is root–3rd–7th; Form B is root–7th–3rd. When roots fall a 5th you alternate A and B, and the guide tones move smoothly by themselves.

5From shell to full voicing, one layer at a time

Jazz voicings stack in layers, each built on the one before. Pick a chord and build it up; each layer plays as it lands.

  1. Guide tones (3 + 7): 2 notes, the bare identity of the chord.
  2. Shell (R + 3 + 7): 3 notes, for a pianist with no bassist or a big-band guitarist.
  3. Rootless (3, 7 + two extensions): 4 notes, when a bassist covers the root.
  4. Shell + upper-structure triad: the shell in the left hand and a triad in the right. This is the idea behind your wheels.

6Intervals: the building blocks underneath

An interval is the distance between two notes. Every chord, scale, voicing and progression above is a pattern of intervals. Pick one to hear it and see where it shows up.

Chords are stacked 3rds

Every seventh chord is three 3rds stacked up; only the order of major (M) and minor (m) 3rds changes. Remove the bottom 3rd and what's left is the upper triad from your wheels.

Inversion: flip an interval upside down

An interval and its inversion add up to an octave. Major becomes minor, perfect stays perfect, and the numbers add to 9. That's why shell Form A spaces the guide tones a 5th apart and Form B a 4th apart, and why the tritone (6 + 6 = 12) makes tritone substitution work: G7's B–F is D♭7's F–C♭.

Compound intervals and the avoid note

Extensions are compound intervals: 9 = 2nd + octave, 11 = 4th + octave, 13 = 6th + octave. A minor 9th above a chord tone sounds harsh. The natural 11 on a major chord puts F a minor 9th above E, so it's avoided; on a minor chord F sits a major 9th above E♭, which sounds fine.

7Put it in your hands

  1. Comping: play only the 3rd and 7th in your right hand with the root in the bass. Clean, and it leaves room for extensions or a singer.
  2. Soloing: aim for a guide tone on the downbeat when a chord changes. Landing on B over G13, then E over Cmaj13, spells out the changes with no band behind you.
  3. Writing: either guide-tone line makes a strong melody, bass line or string part.
  4. Ear: loop the progression and sing just the upper voice (B A G G F F E E), then the lower one. Use the Spotlight menu at the top to bring one voice forward.

Practical notes

  • Register: keep the 3rd and 7th roughly between C3 and C5. Below about D3 they turn muddy.
  • Bud Powell shells: the two-note version (root + 7th, or root + 3rd/10th) is a classic bebop left-hand sound.
  • Guitar: the same shapes are the classic "Freddie Green" voicings: root on the 6th or 5th string, 3rd and 7th on the next two strings.
